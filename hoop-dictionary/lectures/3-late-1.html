3.13: Late Clock, Offense

Chapter 3: Offense

When the clock, not the defense, is the constraint: the late-clock possession and the 2-for-1.

New terms introduced (2): 2-for-1, Late clock. Diagrams: 1.

This chapter has treated the defense as the thing an offense is working against. In the last seconds of a shot clock or a quarter, it is not. The constraint is the clock, and the clock changes which shot is a good shot. A contested mid-range jumper with two seconds left is not a bad decision; it is the best remaining option, and judging it against the shot an offense could have had with eighteen seconds left is a category error.

This lecture is short by design. It has two entries and no diagrams of actions, because nothing new happens on the floor: the actions are the ones already taught, run faster and with fewer options. What changes is the arithmetic.

1 The late-clock possession

Late clock is the state, not an action: the shot clock is under about seven seconds and the offense has to produce a shot rather than an advantage. Three things follow.

The order of preference inverts. For most of a possession an offense passes up a contested two to find an open three or a layup. Late, a contested two taken with two seconds left is worth more than a better shot that does not get taken, because a shot-clock violation is worth nothing at all.

The actions shrink. There is no time for the motion offense of lecture 3.11 to produce anything, so teams run a ball screen or an isolation: one action, one read, a shot. The snake and the step-up screen of lecture 3.3 are common here because each produces a shot from one dribble.

The offensive rebound is worth more. A missed shot with one second on the shot clock and fifteen on the game clock is a live ball with a fresh fourteen if it is tipped out, which is why teams send more players to the glass late than they would otherwise. That is the other half of the crash or get back decision of lecture 4.1.

2 The 2-for-1

The 2-for-1 is arithmetic rather than tactics, as in Figure 1. Near the end of a quarter, if the offense shoots early enough, it will get the ball back for a second possession before the period ends; if it does not, the opponent gets the last shot and the offense gets one. The rule of thumb is that with about thirty-five seconds left, a shot taken by about twenty-eight seconds leaves time for the opponent’s possession and a final one of your own.

That is the end of the offense. Chapter 4 takes the same possession from the other side, in the same order: transition defense first, then defending early offense, then the coverages built for the ball screens of lectures 3.3 and 3.4, the help and rotations that answer the drives and cuts of lectures 3.7 and 3.10, the switching schemes that answer the screens of lectures 3.5 and 3.6, zones, presses, and the defensive halves of the special situations and the late clock. Every “beaten by” slot in this chapter names the lecture in Chapter 4 where its coverage is taught.
