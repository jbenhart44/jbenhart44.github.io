3.8: Sets and Systems

Chapter 3: Offense

The alignment a team starts from and the script it runs: Horns, Zipper, the set play as a concept, and the named sets and plays heard that have no entry yet.

New terms introduced (3): Horns, Set play, Zipper. Diagrams: 2.

Five lectures of actions have treated each action as a thing on its own. A team does not run one action; it starts from a shape and runs a sequence. The shape is the set, and the sequence is the play. This lecture is about both, and about the difference, because the two words are used interchangeably on a broadcast and mean different things.

A set is where the five players stand when the possession starts: Horns with two bigs at the elbows, 5-out with everyone outside the arc, a box with four players in a square. It carries no instructions. What it does is decide which actions are available and which help is far away, and a good set makes several different plays look identical for the first two seconds.

A set play is the script: this screen, then that cut, then this pass, run to get one shot for one player. It is the opposite of the motion offense and flow of lecture 3.11, which give players rules instead of a script.

Section 1 is Horns, the most common set in the sport and the clearest example of what a set is for. Section 2 is Zipper, an action named for the cut it contains, which teams run out of several sets. Section 3 is the set play as a concept: why teams run them, when, and what they cost. Section 4 names the sets and plays heard on a broadcast for which this site has a description but not an entry.

1 Horns

Horns is the point guard at the top with the ball, two bigs at the two elbows, and two shooters in the two corners, as in Figure 1. Every glossary that names it names the same shape. What it buys is stated by looking at the floor: the paint is empty, so any drive meets nobody; the two bigs are both a pass away from the ball and both able to screen it; the two shooters are in the corners, the furthest a help defender can be from the rim and still be guarding someone who can shoot.

From that shape, several plays start the same way and end differently. Figure 2 shows one of them: the ball-handler takes a ball screen from one big and drives away from it, while the other big, instead of rolling, sets a flare screen for the corner shooter lifting to the wing, and the skip pass goes there. The first two seconds are identical to a plain pick-and-roll, and the defense has to guard both.

2 Zipper

The zipper is an action rather than a set: a player cuts from the block straight up the lane line off a screen and catches at the slot, as in Figure 3. It is a pin-down with the cut made vertically along the lane line rather than out toward the wing, and what it buys is a catch at the top with the whole floor in front of the catcher rather than a catch on the wing with half of it. Teams run it as the first action of a possession, to get the ball into the hands of a guard who did not bring it up, and as an entry into a ball screen: the catcher at the slot is immediately in position for the big to screen for him.

3 The set play

A set play is a script. Its value is that five players know what is coming and the defense does not, and its cost is that it takes three or four seconds to set up, during which the defense is getting organized. That trade decides when teams run them: after a timeout, when the defense cannot have prepared for this one; at the start of a possession against a set defense, when nothing is available anyway; for a specific player who needs a specific shot; and at the end of a quarter or a game, when there is one possession and no second chance. The rest of the time most teams would rather play with rules, which is lecture 3.11.

4 Names heard for which there is no entry yet

The sets and plays below are named by one publisher, or are one team’s call for an action this site already teaches under another name. Each has a description here and no entry; the rule and the dated diff are in data/sources/glossary_diff_2026-09-21.md.

  • Horns twist. A Horns play in which the two elbow bigs screen for each other before one of them screens the ball, so the ball screen arrives from the side the defense did not expect.
  • 1-4 set, box set, double stack. Three more alignments. The 1-4 has both bigs at the elbows and both wings in the corners, which flattens the defense and opens the lane for back cuts; the box is four players in a square at the elbows and blocks; the double stack puts two players on each block. Box and stack are taught in lecture 3.12, where they are used most.
  • Miami. A combination in which the ball is entered to the high post, the point guard sets a shuffle screen, and the shuffle screener then takes a handoff from the high post.
  • Corner offense. An alignment with three players on the strong side (a big at the elbow, a guard in the corner and one on the wing), a big at the weak-side elbow, and a forward near the weak-side slot.
  • Team-named plays. The plays pages of the coaching sites are full of calls named for teams and colleges. Those are one team’s name for a sequence, not vocabulary, and they are out of scope for this site by rule.

The idea to carry into lecture 3.9 is that a set play ends. The offenses in the next lecture do not: they are continuities, which return to their own starting shape and can be run until the shot clock ends.
