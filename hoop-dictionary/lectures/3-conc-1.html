3.11: Motion, Conceptual Offense, Reads and Counters

Chapter 3: Offense

Offense run from rules rather than a script: motion, read and react, isolation and the mismatch, and the synthesis of every beaten-by slot in this chapter.

New terms introduced (4): Isolation, Mismatch, Motion offense, Read and react. Diagrams: 4.

Nine lectures have named actions. This one is about the offenses that do not call them. A set play tells five players what to do; a conceptual offense tells them what to do if. The instructions are rules attached to events: if the ball is passed, screen away; if a teammate drives at you, cut behind him; if you are denied, back cut. Nobody calls anything, and the actions of lectures 3.2 to 3.9 appear anyway, because the rules produce them.

Section 1 is motion offense, the oldest of these. Section 2 is read and react, the most systematic. Section 3 is the two situations in which an offense deliberately stops moving: isolation and the mismatch. Section 4 is the synthesis this chapter has been building toward: every “beaten by” slot in Chapter 3 is a coverage from Chapter 4, and choosing the action that beats the coverage being shown is what an offense actually does.

1 Motion offense

Motion offense gives each player a small set of rules about what to do when the ball moves. The classic pair is pass and screen away and fill the spot behind, as in Figure 1. The ball goes from the slot to the wing; the passer screens for someone on the far side rather than cutting toward the ball; the man he screens comes up to fill the spot the passer left. The floor is back in its shape, the ball is on a new side, and the rules apply again.

2 Read and react

Read and react attaches the rules to the drive rather than the pass, and it is the most explicit version of a conceptual offense: each player has a specified reaction to each thing the ball does, learned in layers, as in Figure 2. A baseline drive makes the man behind it circle to the spot it left, the weak-side corner lift to the wing, and the players away from the ball move up a spot. A drive at a teammate makes him cut behind it, which is the 45 cut of lecture 3.7. A pass to the post makes the passer and the nearest teammate cut in opposite directions.

3 When the offense stops moving

Two situations are worth stopping for.

Isolation clears one side of the floor so one attacker can work alone, as in Figure 3. Everything this chapter has built exists to make two defenders guard one attacker; an isolation is the admission that this particular attacker beats one defender by himself, and the four teammates are there only to keep their own defenders too far away to help. It is the least collective thing an offense does, and it is correct whenever the individual advantage is larger than the advantage a screen would create.

A mismatch is the same idea created by the defense rather than chosen by the offense. After a switch, a big is guarding a guard or a guard is guarding a big, as in Figure 4. Both are attackable and each has its own route: the guard drives the slower defender, or the big posts the smaller one, which is the post-up and the early seal of lecture 3.7. The defense’s answer is the scram switch of lecture 4.5, which is why the early seal has to be early.

4 The synthesis: read the coverage, pick the action

Every card in this chapter ends with a “beaten by” slot, and every one of those slots names a coverage from Chapter 4. Reading them the other way round gives the offense’s actual decision procedure, because a coverage that beats one action is usually what makes another one available:

  • The defense drops on ball screens, conceding the pull-up to protect the rim. Then the pop and the pull-up are open, and the snake gets to them.
  • The defense plays at the level or hedges, killing the pull-up. Then the slip and the ghost punish the early commitment, and the rocket pass beats the hedge.
  • The defense traps, taking the ball out of the best player’s hands. Then the short roll and the 4-on-3 are the whole answer.
  • The defense ices toward the sideline. Then the step-up screen sends the handler baseline instead, and the twist re-screens him back to the middle.
  • The defense switches everything. Then screens stop mattering and mismatch, early seal and isolation are what is left, which is why switching teams need five players who can guard.
  • The defense top-locks shooters off screens. Then the back cut is open behind the lock, every time.
  • The defense helps hard from the weak side. Then the hammer, the kick-out and the skip pass are the punishment.
  • The defense never helps. Then the drive and the isolation are the offense, because nobody is guarding two players.

This is what a good offense is doing while it looks like it is running a play: showing an action, seeing the coverage, and running the action that beats it. The named actions of lectures 3.4 and 3.6 exist for exactly this reason, which is why each of them was introduced with the coverage it was invented against.

The idea to carry into lecture 3.12 is that all of this assumes a live ball and a set defense. The next lecture is about the possessions that start from neither: out of bounds, after a timeout, against a press, and against a zone.
