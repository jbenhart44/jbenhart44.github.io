3.5: Off-Ball Screens I: The Screen Types

Chapter 3: Offense

Pin-down, flare, back screen, rip, cross screen, stagger: the six shapes a screen away from the ball can take, and the four reads a cutter makes off any of them.

New terms introduced (8): Back screen, Chin, Cross screen, Double screen, Flare screen, Pin-down, Rip screen, Stagger. Diagrams: 8.

A ball screen frees the player who already has the ball. An off-ball screen frees the player who is about to get it, and that is a different problem, because the defense is not watching him. A defender guarding a player without the ball stands between his man and the ball, with his head turned to see both, and everything about an off-ball screen is designed around where that defender is looking. A screen he can see coming, he can avoid. A screen set on his blind side, or set while he is turned toward the ball, he cannot.

The six screens in this lecture are the shapes. What distinguishes them is the direction the screen faces and the direction the cutter goes: up toward the perimeter, away along the arc, back toward the rim, across the lane. The named actions of lecture 3.6 are built by combining two or three of these; this lecture is the parts list. Section 1 is the pin-down and the four reads a cutter makes off it, which are the same four reads off every screen here. Section 2 is the flare screen, for a defender who cheats toward the ball. Section 3 is the back screen and the rip screen, both set behind a defender who is watching the ball. Section 4 is the cross screen, the big man’s screen. Section 5 is the stagger, two screens for one cutter. Section 6 is two names built from these parts: the double screen, two screeners side by side, and the chin, a back screen at the weak-side elbow.

1 The pin-down, and the four reads

The pin-down is the most common off-ball screen in the sport, and the one every shooter learns first. A shooter starts near the baseline, usually on the block or in the corner. A screener stands above him, facing the baseline, and the shooter runs up off the screen to the wing or the slot for a catch, as in Figure 1. It is also called a down screen, because the screener has come down toward the baseline to set it.

What makes the pin-down teachable is that the cutter has the same four choices off it that he has off every off-ball screen, and the choice is decided by what his defender does:

  • Curl. The defender chases the cutter over the top of the screen, right behind him. The cutter curls the screen, turning it into a cut toward the rim, and the defender is trailing.
  • Fade. The defender goes under the screen, between the screener and the rim, to meet the cutter at the wing. The cutter fades the other way, away from the ball toward the corner, and the defender has gone the wrong side of the screen.
  • Straight cut. The defender is caught on the screen. The cutter comes straight up to the wing and shoots.
  • Back cut. The defender anticipates and jumps above the screen to deny the catch at the wing. The cutter reverses and goes back door to the rim behind him, the back cut of lecture 3.7.

One name heard for a version of the pin-down has no entry of its own on this site. A wide pin-down is set in the corner or the short corner for a cutter who starts in the corner, so the cut is longer and the catch is at the wing. Two more names are built from the screens in this lecture and have entries in Section 6: the double screen, two screeners shoulder to shoulder, which is the stagger of Section 5 with the screeners side by side instead of one behind the other; and the chin, a back screen at the weak-side elbow.

2 The flare screen

The flare screen is for the defender who cheats toward the ball. A perimeter player is standing at the top or the slot, and his defender, watching the ball on the wing, has drifted a step toward it. A screener steps into the path the defender would take to follow his man away from the ball, and the man fades along the arc to the far wing, as in Figure 2. The pass is a skip pass over the top. The flare is the pin-down turned ninety degrees: the cutter goes sideways along the arc rather than up from the baseline, and the screen is set on the side of the defender that faces away from the ball.

3 The back screen and the rip

The back screen is set where the defender cannot see it. A defender guarding a player away from the ball stands between him and the ball, facing the ball. The screener comes up behind that defender and sets the screen on his back, and the man he is guarding cuts to the rim, as in Figure 3. The defender learns about the screen when he tries to follow his man and cannot. The pass is a lob or a bounce pass to the block. The UCLA cut of lecture 3.7 is a back screen set at the elbow; the Spain pick-and-roll of lecture 3.4 is a back screen set on the roller’s defender.

The rip screen is a back screen with a particular cutter: the player who has just set a screen himself. A big sets a pin-down or a ball screen; his defender is now above him toward the ball, having helped on the cutter; a second player comes across and back-screens that defender, and the big rips to the rim, as in Figure 4. It is the simplest form of screening the screener, and it works because a defender who has just helped on a screen is standing exactly where a back screen wants him.

4 The cross screen

The cross screen is set across the lane, block to block, and it is the screen that gets a big man a post catch. Two bigs are on the two blocks; one comes across and screens the other’s defender; the other cuts across the lane to the ball-side block, seals his man, and catches the entry pass, as in Figure 5. The ball usually arrives by reversal: it is on the far wing when the screen is set and comes to the near wing as the cutter arrives. Because the screen is set low, the cutter’s defender has to choose between going over it, which puts him behind the cutter, and going under it, which puts him under the rim and out of the passing lane.

5 The stagger

The stagger is two screens in a row in the same direction for one cutter, as in Figure 6. Two screeners stand one above the other along the cutter’s path, and the cutter runs off both. The defender has to get around two bodies rather than one, and if he chooses the wrong side of the first screen he is on the wrong side of the second. The stagger is the base of most of the named off-ball actions in lecture 3.6: the floppy is a single screen one way and a stagger the other, the loop is a stagger along the baseline.

6 Two names built from these parts: the double screen and the chin

The double screen is the stagger turned sideways (Figure 7). The two screeners stand shoulder to shoulder instead of one behind the other, so the cutter’s defender meets a wall twice as wide as one screen. A defender can shade a single screen, cheating over the top or slipping under; against two bodies side by side he has no shade to play, and going around the end costs him a step.

The chin is a back screen set at the weak-side elbow for the player in the slot (Figure 8). It is the back screen of Section 3 in a particular place: the slot defender on the weak side is watching the ball with the elbow behind him, so the screen lands on a defender who cannot see it, and the cut goes to the rim through space the ball side has already emptied. The two glossaries that name it agree on that screen and disagree on how much it covers. One treats the back screen as the whole action; the other treats it as one part of a Princeton-family set, with the post at the high post and a combination of back screens, ball screens and flare screens. The card below is the part they share.

7 Names heard for which there is no entry yet

Five more off-ball actions are named on the glossaries this site draws on, each by one publisher only. Under this site’s rule a name like that has a description here and no entry until a second independent source names it, so that the word is not a mystery when it is heard. The rule is in the plan (plan/PLAN.md, section 8.4) and the dated report behind these dispositions is data/sources/glossary_diff_2026-09-22.md.

  • Power. A cross screen set in the lane by one post player for the other. It is the cross screen of Section 4 with a call attached to it, so a team that says power is asking for that screen and not for a new action.
  • Thunder. A pin-down set for a big man, who comes off it from one block to the opposite elbow rather than out to the perimeter. The screen is the one in Section 1; what the name adds is who cuts and where he finishes.
  • Veer. A big sprints out as if to set a ball screen for the point guard, then veers away and sets a pin-down for a player in the corner instead. The ball screen is only the approach. The screen that actually gets set is the pin-down, on a different defender, at the other end of the big’s run.
  • Twirl. Two publishers name this word and describe different actions with it. On one, a cutter curls a screen and the ball goes straight into the screener as soon as the cutter has cleared. On the other, the cutter who curled turns and screens for the man who just screened for him, which is the screen-the-screener idea the rip of Section 3 is built on. Until the two agree, the word has a description here and not a card.
  • Snap. Described as twirl with one change: the ball is thrown into the post rather than to the screener stepping out to the perimeter.

The idea to carry into lecture 3.6 is that these six screens are a parts list. The actions that carry names on a broadcast are built from two or three of them in sequence, run for a particular player, and the next lecture takes the most common of those apart into the parts you have just seen.
