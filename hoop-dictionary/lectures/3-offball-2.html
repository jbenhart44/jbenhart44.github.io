3.6: Off-Ball Screens II: Named Off-Ball Actions

Chapter 3: Offense

Elevator, Hammer, Floppy, Loop and Wedge: the named actions built from the six screen shapes, each taken apart into its parts, plus the names heard that have no entry yet.

New terms introduced (5): Elevator screen, Floppy, Hammer, Loop, Wedge. Diagrams: 5.

Lecture 3.5 was a parts list. This lecture is what teams build from it. A named off-ball action is two or three of those screens run in a sequence, for a particular player, with a particular pass at the end, and the name is shorthand a team uses so that five players can start it with one word. The names below are the ones heard most often on a broadcast, and each is taken apart here into the screens it is made of, so that a reader who has the parts can recognize any combination, named or not.

Section 1 is the elevator, two screens that close. Section 2 is the hammer, a back screen on the weak side of a drive. Section 3 is floppy, a shooter’s choice of sides. Section 4 is the loop, a stagger run along the baseline. Section 5 is the wedge, an off-ball screen that becomes a ball screen. Section 6 names the actions heard for which this site has a description but not yet an entry.

1 Elevator: two screens that close

The elevator screen is two screeners standing side by side with a gap between them. The shooter runs through the gap, and the moment he is through the two screeners step together and close it, so the defender chasing him runs into two bodies with no way round, as in Figure 1 and Figure 2. It is also called elevator doors. It is the only screen in the sport that is set after the cutter has passed it, and it is legal only if both screeners are stationary when the defender arrives, which is why the timing of the step is everything.

2 Hammer: the back screen on the weak side of a drive

The hammer joins a drive to a screen on the other side of the floor. The ball-handler drives baseline on one side. The weak-side shooter’s defender does what every help defender is taught to do and sinks toward the paint to stop the drive. As he sinks, a big back-screens him, and the shooter drifts down to the weak-side corner. The driver throws the pass along the baseline, under the rim, to the corner, as in Figure 3 and Figure 4. The hammer punishes the help rule itself: the defender who helped is screened for having helped.

3 Floppy: the shooter picks a side

Floppy gives a shooter a choice. He starts under the rim. On one side of him is a single pin-down; on the other is a stagger. He reads his defender and runs off whichever side is open, as in Figure 5. The defender, who has to guard both sides at once, cannot be right about both, and the passer at the top waits for the read and delivers to whichever wing the shooter chose.

4 Loop: a stagger along the baseline

The loop is a triple stagger run along the baseline. The cutter starts on one wing, runs down to the baseline, under the rim off three screens set along the lane, and up to the other wing for the catch, as in Figure 6. It is the longest cut in this chapter, and its length is the point: three screeners in a row along a path the defender has to run in full, with the ball waiting at the top for the catch at the far end.

5 Wedge: the off-ball screen that becomes a ball screen

The wedge is where the two kinds of screen meet. A guard sets an off-ball screen for the big, who cuts up to the top; as the big arrives he turns and sets a ball screen for the handler, as in Figure 7. The big’s defender has been screened once and is now late to a second screen, which is the same idea as the ram of lecture 3.4 with the first screen coming from a different direction: the ram pins the big down from above, the wedge screens him from the side.

6 Names heard for which there is no entry yet

The following named off-ball actions are described by one publisher only. Under this site’s rule they have a description here and no card until a second independent source names them. The diff that decided this is data/sources/glossary_diff_2026-09-21.md.

  • Gaggle. A stagger in which the first screener, after screening, cuts to the opposite corner; the shooter runs through the screens and pops out for the shot. One glossary calls the screens split screens.
  • Baseline stagger. A single or double stagger set along the baseline for a shooter who fills the corner rather than coming up to the wing.
  • Picket fence. Three screeners in a line along the baseline, and a shooter who runs through all three, which is a loop with the screens set closer together.
  • Screen your own. Against a switching defense, a player screens his own defender so that defender cannot switch onto the cutter going by.
  • Fade. Turning down a screen and cutting away from it, which lecture 3.5 taught as one of the four reads rather than as an action of its own.
  • Hook. A cutter curls off a pin-down, and the screener who set it turns around and sets a ball screen. It joins an off-ball screen to a ball screen the way the wedge of Section 5 does, with the roles swapped: the wedge’s ball screen is set by the player who came off the first screen, and here it is set by the player who set that first screen.
  • Moon. A cutter starts in the middle of the floor and runs off a stagger out to the wing. The screens are the stagger of lecture 3.5, and what the name adds is the path, from the middle of the floor out to the wing rather than wing to wing.
  • Tops. A down screen at the top of the key, then the ball reversed to the other side, then a back screen for the cutter. It is three pieces in order rather than one action, and all three of them come from lecture 3.5.

The idea to carry into lecture 3.7 is that not every off-ball movement is a screen. The next lecture is about cuts, seals and handoffs: what a player without the ball does to get open when nobody screens for him.
