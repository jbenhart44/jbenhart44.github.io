1.4: The Three-Point Line, 1979 to 2001

Chapter 1: History and eras

The line’s slow adoption, Bird and Magic, the physical 1990s, Jordan, and the illegal-defense rules as they stood until 2001.

No new terms introduced. No diagrams.

A rule can exist for twenty years before anyone uses it properly. That is the story of this era: the three-point line arrived in 1979 and the sport spent two decades treating it as a specialty shot rather than as the thing an offense should be organized around. What the era is remembered for instead is two rookies, one dynasty, and the most physical defense the league has allowed.

The end date of this era is a stated gap. The NBA rulebook on file states the defensive three-second rule as it now stands, in Rule No. 10, Section VII: “any defensive player, who is positioned in the 16-foot lane or the area extending 4 feet past the lane endline, must be actively guarding an opponent within three seconds.”1 It does not state when that rule was adopted, and NBA.com’s season review of 2001-02 does not mention a rule change at all.2 So the RULE has a primary source and the DATE does not. The boundary is drawn at 2001 on the strength of secondary accounts that are not on file, and under this site’s rule (owner decision 2) a boundary that rests on secondary sources holds as a gap rather than shipping as a fact. What would close it: the league’s own announcement of the 2001 rule change, or a rulebook edition that carries its adoption date.

1 The line nobody used

The rule that defines the era arrived complete and was ignored for most of it. What makes this striking is that the arithmetic was available from the first day: a shot worth three is worth taking at a lower success rate than a shot worth two, and the ratio is not subtle. The reason it took twenty years is not that anyone miscalculated but that nobody was counting, which is what lecture 2.2 is about.

The consequence for the vocabulary of this site is that most of Chapter 3 would have looked strange for most of this era. 5-out spacing, the corner three as a designed shot, the kick-out as the main assist: all of them presume the line is worth organizing around.

2 Bird and Magic

The two rookies of 1979-80 are the players the era is named for by everyone except this chapter.

NBA.com’s review of that first season records what he did to earn it, playing the center position in the deciding game with Abdul-Jabbar injured: “In Game 6 of the 1980 Finals, Magic Johnson scores 42 points playing the center position to win the NBA title.”9

3 The physical 1990s and Jordan

The back half of the era is the most physical defense the league has permitted, and the player who defined it is the one who had to play through it.

The defensive rules of this era are the reason isolation was a primary offense rather than a late-clock option. A defense was not permitted to play what would now be called a zone: the illegal-defense rules required defenders to be guarding someone, which meant an attacker who cleared one side of the floor genuinely had it to himself. The isolation of lecture 3.11 is what an offense does when the rules guarantee no help arrives from the weak side.

4 What changed in 2001

The rules that ended the era did two things. They removed the prohibition on illegal defense, which permitted zone defenses in the professional game for the first time. And they introduced the defensive three-second rule, which prevented a defender from simply standing in the lane to take the rim away, by requiring him to be “actively guarding an opponent within three seconds.”13

Those two changes together produce the game Chapter 3 describes. A defense may now be anywhere, so long as nobody parks in the paint; an offense must therefore create space rather than be handed it; and spacing, the drive and the kick-out become the organizing ideas. That is the subject of lecture 1.5.
