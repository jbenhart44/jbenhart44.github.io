4.9: Late Clock, Defense

Chapter 4: Defense

The foul as a tactic: the foul game that trades free throws for possessions, fouling up three, and hack-a, with what the rule book did to the last of these.

New terms introduced (2): Foul game, Hack-a. No diagrams.

Lecture 3.13 was about what the offense does when the clock, not the defense, is the constraint. This lecture is the mirror, and it is short by design, because the defense’s late-clock tools are few and all of them are the same tool: the foul. In the last minutes of a close game a defense stops trying to prevent a score and starts trying to buy a possession, and the price it pays is free throws. Every tactic here is a version of that trade, and every one of them is an argument about arithmetic rather than about guarding anybody.

Section 1 is the foul game, the trailing team’s trade. Section 2 is its inverse, fouling when ahead. Section 3 is hack-a, the trade made away from the ball, and the rule that changed its price.

1 The foul game

A team that is behind late needs possessions, and the clock is taking them away. The foul game is the deliberate foul on the ball to stop the clock and put the leading team at the free-throw line: the defense concedes one or two free throws, which can be missed, and receives in return a possession it would otherwise have spent watching the clock run. It is a bet on three things at once: that the shooter will miss, that the trailing team will score on its own end, and that there are enough possessions left for the first two to matter. The bet is usually bad, which is why it is made only when the alternative is certain, and it is why a team that is up by a lot in the last minute wants the ball in the hands of its best free-throw shooter.

The transition take foul of lecture 4.1 was the same trade in a different place, and the rule book’s answer to it was to make the trade worse for the defense: one free throw and the offense keeps the ball. The foul game on a set possession is still priced the old way, two free throws and a change of possession, because the rules treat it as an ordinary personal foul, and that is the only reason it survives.

2 Fouling up three

The trade runs the other way when the defense is ahead. A team that leads by three in the last seconds can foul before the trailing team shoots, and give it two free throws, which are worth at most two points, instead of a three-point attempt that would tie the game. The offense’s only recovery is to make the first free throw, miss the second on purpose, win the rebound and score again, which is three unlikely things in a row. Coaches disagree about when it is worth it: a foul with too much time left hands the possession back with a two-point lead still in play, and a foul on a shooter already in his motion is three free throws. The window is a few seconds wide, and the tactic is a rule a team decides on before the game, not a decision made on the floor.

It is the defense’s version of the two-for-one of lecture 3.13: a play whose only justification is the arithmetic of the clock.

3 Hack-a

Hack-a is the foul game played away from the ball. Instead of fouling the handler, the defense fouls a poor free-throw shooter who does not have the ball, at any point in the possession, and sends him to the line for two. If he makes fewer than half, the defense has held the offense to less than a point per possession without guarding it, and it has done so as many times as it has fouls to spend. The glossary on file gives the full name, Hack-a-Shaq, and defines it as “intentionally fouling the opposition’s worst free-throw shooter and sending them to the line”;1 the shorter form is what a broadcast says. The tactic’s own logic is that it works only against a player bad enough at the line that two of his free throws are worth less than one ordinary possession.

The NBA priced this out of most of the game with the away-from-the-play foul. The penalty section of the current rule reads: “A personal foul and team foul shall be assessed and one free throw attempt shall be awarded. The free throw may be attempted by any player in the game at the time the personal foul was committed.” and, when the ball is inbounds, “the offended team shall be awarded the ball”.2 One free throw, taken by whoever the offense chooses, and the ball back: the trade the tactic depended on no longer exists in the window the rule covers. Where the window begins is a stated gap on this site, because the definition lives in a section of the rule book not yet on file.

The idea to carry out of this chapter is the one the whole chapter has been building. Every defense is a decision about what to concede: the drop concedes the pull-up, the switch concedes the mismatch, the zone concedes the arc, the press concedes the layup, and the foul game concedes the free throw. A viewer who can name what a defense is conceding can name the defense, and can see, one pass before it happens, where the offense is going to attack. That is what this site set out to make possible, and the Reference is where every one of those names now lives.
