1.3: The Shot-Clock Game, 1954 to 1979

Chapter 1: History and eras

Twenty-five years inside the arc that did not yet exist: Russell and Chamberlain, the Celtics, the ABA, and the line that ends the era.

No new terms introduced. No diagrams.

The longest era in this chapter, and the one that most resembles the game as a set of principles rather than as a set of shots. Every possession now had a deadline; nothing yet rewarded shooting from distance. What the sport did with those twenty-five years is work out how to score near the rim against a defense that knew you had to.

1 What a possession without a three-point line is

A modern viewer reading Chapter 3 might assume spacing is a permanent feature of basketball. It is not; it is a consequence of the three-point line, and this era is the proof. If every shot is worth two points, a shot from twenty-three feet is a worse version of a shot from ten, and there is no reason for an attacker to stand beyond the arc except to make room. Gap as a measurement means little when no defender is obliged to guard a man twenty-five feet from the rim.

So the offense of this era crowds the paint on purpose, the post-up is the central action, and the defense is built to stop shots close to the basket. The cross screen of lecture 3.5 and the high-low of lecture 3.7 are this era’s characteristic actions, and both exist to get one big man the ball on the block against another.

2 Russell and Chamberlain

The era’s two defining players played the same position in opposite ways, and the league’s own year-by-year award lists record the result.

The Finals MVP entry is worth pausing on, because it is a case where a count means less than it looks. NBA.com’s page states that “since 1969, the NBA has honored the NBA Finals’ best performer”,7 so a player whose best seasons were in the early 1960s could not have won it however he played. This is the clearest reason this chapter counts honors within an era rather than comparing totals across eras: the things being counted did not exist for the same length of time.

3 Abdul-Jabbar, and why a career is not an era

Kareem Abdul-Jabbar’s six Most Valuable Player awards are the most on the league’s list, and they fall on both sides of this era’s ending: three with Milwaukee in the early 1970s and three with the Lakers, the last of them in 1979-80, the first season of the next era.8

That span is the argument for how this chapter is organized. If eras were defined by who was playing, Abdul-Jabbar’s career would be one era, and it would include both the game before the three-point line and the game after it. Because eras here are defined by dated rule changes, his career crosses a boundary, which is what a career is supposed to do.

4 The ABA and the merger

The rival American Basketball Association operated through the back half of this era and ended in 1976, when four of its teams joined the NBA. The league’s own review of the 1976-77 season names them: “Four teams from the disbanded ABA — the New York Nets, Indiana Pacers, Denver Nuggets and San Antonio Spurs — became NBA franchises.”12 The rest of the ABA’s players were allocated by a dispersal draft, and the same page follows two of them, Artis Gilmore to Chicago and Julius Erving to Philadelphia.13 Its lasting contribution is the line that ends this lecture: the three-point shot was an ABA feature before it was an NBA one.

The merger’s terms are still not sourced here, only its outcome. The 1976-77 recap acquired on 2026-09-22 closes the part of this gap that mattered most, which was that the site asserted a merger with nothing on file: the four teams are now named by the league’s own page. What that page does not give is the agreement behind it, the money, or why four teams and not more, and none of the recaps on file states them. Those remain unsourced and are therefore not claimed.

5 The line

The era ends with a rule adopted from the competitor that had just folded into it. NBA.com’s review of 1979-80 records it plainly: “the 3-point field goal, a popular facet of the ABA game, was adopted by the league.”14 The same page notes what everyone remembers about that season instead: it “would forever be known as the year Larry Bird and Earvin” Magic Johnson arrived.15

Both things are true, and the ordering is the point of this chapter. The rookies are what the season is remembered for; the line is what changed the sport. The idea to carry into lecture 1.4 is that it did not change it at once: the next lecture is about the twenty years in which the line was on the floor and almost nobody shot from behind it.
