4.8: Special Situations, Defense

Chapter 4: Defense

Defending the baseline and sideline inbounds: pressure the passer and the five-second count, switch everything, zone the inbound; and guarding the after-timeout play.

No new terms introduced. No diagrams.

Lecture 3.12 described the offense’s special situations as the moments when a coach gets to draw a play and know the defense cannot move until the ball does. The BLOB, the SLOB and the after-timeout play are the offense’s best-rehearsed possessions, and this lecture is about the defense’s side of them. It has fewer options than the offense, because the offense chose the moment, and its whole task is to make the rehearsed play into an unrehearsed one.

There are no new terms here, on purpose, for the same reason as in lecture 4.2: every answer is a coverage from earlier in the chapter applied to a situation from Chapter 3. Section 1 is the passer and the count, which is the one advantage the defense has. Section 2 is switching everything, the modern default. Section 3 is zoning the inbound. Section 4 is the after-timeout play, where the problem is different.

1 The passer and the count

The inbounder is the only attacker who cannot move, and the rule gives him five seconds: in the NBA’s words, a thrower-in shall not “fail to release the ball within 5 seconds”, on penalty of “Loss of ball.”1 Both are the defense’s to use. A defender on the inbounder, arms up and active, takes away the angles the play was drawn with: the lob over the top, the bounce pass under a screen, the direct pass to a cutter’s hands. The defense does not need to steal the inbound; it needs to make the inbounder throw his second choice. And the count runs whether the play works or not, so a defense that denies the first two options has made the inbounder choose between a bad pass and a turnover with the clock at four.

On a baseline inbound the passer’s defender has one more decision: whether to guard the passer or to sag off him and help on the cutters. Guarding him costs a defender at the rim. Leaving him gives the offense five options instead of four. Most teams guard him against a team that lobs and leave him against a team that runs its shooter off screens, and the choice is scouted, not improvised.

2 Switch everything

An inbound play is a set of screens run in a small space with a stopped defense, and the coverage that makes a screen a non-event is the switch. Most defenses at every level switch every screen on a baseline inbound: the two defenders in each screen trade, nobody chases, and the cutter who comes off a screen finds a defender already where he is going. The mismatches a switch creates are a smaller problem here than anywhere else on the floor, because the possession is a few seconds old and the offense has drawn up a play, not a post-up.

Switching is also the answer to the box set and the stack set of lecture 3.12, which exist to make screens collide in a small space. Against a switching defense the collision produces nothing, and the offense’s counter is the slip: a screener who never sets the screen and cuts before the switch has been called, which is the same counter that beat the switch in lecture 4.5.

3 Zone the inbound

The other answer is not to guard the cutters at all. A defense that zones the baseline inbound forms a small zone under the basket, one defender in front of the rim, two flanking him at the edge of the lane, two at the elbows, and guards the spots the play’s cuts are going to rather than the players making them (Figure 1). A BLOB is drawn against man-to-man: its screens are set to free a specific cutter from a specific defender. Against a zone, the cutter comes off his screen free and arrives at a spot that already has a defender standing on it, and the play has produced an open man in a place the defense was guarding anyway.

The zone concedes what every zone concedes, the outside shot: a shooter who steps out to the arc off the inbound is guarded by the elbow defender closing out, not by a man on his hip. Teams zone the inbound against opponents who lob and cut to the rim, and switch against opponents who run shooters to the corners.

4 The after-timeout play

The after-timeout play is a different problem, because the ball is usually inbounded from the sideline or already live, and the offense has had a full timeout to draw something the defense has not seen. There is no count to lean on beyond the usual one and no small space to zone. What the defense has is the same timeout, and the coaching answer is to use it to decide two things: which coverage the team will play on the first ball screen, and which player it will not let touch the ball.

The second decision is the one that changes the possession. A defense coming out of a timeout will often deny the star the inbound pass entirely, face-guarding him, and let the play be run by whoever is left, on the theory that a set play run by the second option is a worse play than a scramble run by the first. Everything else is the half court of lectures 4.3 to 4.5 with one difference: the defense has agreed in advance what it will do, so the first screen gets the called coverage and not a guess.

The idea to carry into lecture 4.9 is that every situation in this lecture was decided by a clock the offense controlled: five seconds to inbound, a timeout to draw. The last lecture of the chapter is about the clock the defense can use against the offense, at the end of a quarter and at the end of a game.
