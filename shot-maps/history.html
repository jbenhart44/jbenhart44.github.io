Scoring in the NBA: An Overview

The goal of this page is to give a high-level view of league-wide trends and their impact on scoring (and therefore the game in general.) Considering these changes should help us as fans understand and contextualize player performances from era to era.

Surprising Results

"Top 20" is each season's 20 leading scorers; "non-top 20" is the rest of the league that season. Every figure is field goals only (the shot data has no free throws); eFG% counts a made three as one and a half makes. These say what happened, not why.

  1. The top 20 take fewer threes than the non-top 20, and still shoot better. In 28 of 30 seasons the top 20 took a smaller share of their shots as threes than the non-top 20 (not in 2001-02 and 2015-16), and their eFG% was higher in every one of them. In 2025-26, 34.9% of their shots were threes against 42.4%, and their eFG% was 55.0% against 54.5%.
  2. The top 20 shoot better than the non-top 20 in every season. Their eFG% was higher in all 30 seasons: most in 1997-98 (49.9% against 47.5%), least in 2019-20 (53.1% against 53.0%). It is not where they shoot from: if the non-top 20 took the top 20's mix of shots and made each zone at their own percentage, they would still score fewer points per shot in all 30 seasons.
  3. Inside the three-point line, the non-top 20 never shoot better. At the rim, in the paint and from midrange, the non-top 20's field-goal percentage was higher than the top 20's in none of the 30 seasons.
  4. Threes are the closest contest. By era, the top 20's lead in three-point percentage was 0.2, 0.7, 1.0 and 0.9 points, against 1.9 to 4.8 points at the rim, in the paint and from midrange. The non-top 20 shot threes better in 12 of 30 seasons, by at most 2.3 points.
  5. In the Threes and Paint era, the top 20's biggest lead is in the paint, not at the rim. From 2020-21 to 2025-26 they made 48.0% of their shots in the paint outside the restricted area against 43.2%, a lead of 4.8 points. At the rim their lead was 2.3 points, and from midrange 3.6.
  6. The position that takes the fewest threes shoots best. Centers had the highest eFG% of the five positions in every era (48.3%, 51.4%, 56.0% and 58.6%) and took the smallest share of their shots as threes (1.7%, 3.1%, 14.7% and 20.0%). They also made the highest percentage at the rim in every era (60.3%, 62.9%, 67.3% and 69.9%).
  7. Point guards are the worst finishers at the rim. They made the lowest percentage at the rim of the five positions in every era (55.2%, 55.5%, 58.1% and 61.4%).
  8. Midrange shots got more accurate as they disappeared. From the Midrange Era to the Threes and Paint era, the non-top 20 went from 55,266 to 19,784 midrange shots a season, and their midrange percentage rose from 38.8% to 40.9%. The top 20 went from 10,375 to 4,497, and from 40.7% to 44.5%.
  9. The top 20's lead was largest in the Midrange Era. On average a season, their eFG% lead was 1.5 points in the Midrange Era and 1.2, 1.1 and 1.0 points in the eras since. Single seasons vary: since then it was largest in 2005-06, at 2.1 points.
  10. The closest seasons came in a run. The top 20's eFG% lead was under 1 point in 9 of 30 seasons: four in a row from 2007-08 to 2010-11, then 2017-18, 2019-20, 2021-22, 2022-23 and 2025-26. The closest was 2019-20, at 0.15 points.
  11. In 2011-12 everyone shot worse, the non-top 20 most. From 2010-11 to 2011-12 the non-top 20's eFG% fell from 49.9% to 48.6%, and the top 20's from 50.4% to 50.0%.

The Best Scorers Shoot More Midrange Shots than Everybody Else

In each of the seasons considered, the top scorers took a greater share of their shots from midrange than the average NBA player. This doesn't mean that they took more midrange shots than threes in total (in 2025-26, 17.4% of their shots were midrange and 34.9% were threes), but it shows that the midrange game is dying faster for those who aren't the "go-to" guy on their team.

Where the top scorers shoot from

Both groups moved out to the three-point line, but the top 20 moved less. Their mid-range share fell 17.7 points (35.2% in 1996-97 to 17.4% in 2025-26); the non-top 20's fell 19.8 points (28.9% to 9.1%).

Against his own league

Each dot is one season’s scoring leader. Across: how much of his team’s shooting he did. Up: how much better than his league he shot (eFG%, which counts a made three as one and a half makes). Above the gray line, he shot better than his league; below it, worse.

  • The most efficient scoring title against its own league: Stephen Curry in 2015-16, whose eFG% was 12.8 points above the league's.
  • The most volume: Allen Iverson in 2001-02, who took 35.1% of his team's shots.
  • 7 of 30 scoring leaders shot below their league: Michael Jordan 1997-98 (-0.5); Allen Iverson 1998-99 (-2.7); Allen Iverson 2000-01 (-2.6); Allen Iverson 2001-02 (-5.6); Allen Iverson 2004-05 (-3.0); Russell Westbrook 2014-15 (-4.1); Russell Westbrook 2016-17 (-3.9).

Shot Efficiency

In all 30 seasons the top 20 scored more points per shot than the non-top 20. The gap was widest in 1997-98: 0.046 points a shot. These count field goals only: the shot data has no free throws.

Points

The average top-20 scorer went from 22.7 points per game in 1996-97 to 26.2 in 2025-26 (the highest was 27.4, in 2022-23). The 20th-best went from 20.1 to 21.7. The best single season: James Harden, 36.1 in 2018-19.

So what changed?

  • Scoring is up: the average top-20 scorer scores 3.5 more points per game than in 1996-97.
  • The league moved to the three-point line faster than its best scorers did: in 2025-26 the non-top 20 took 42.4% of their shots as threes, the top 20 34.9%.
  • The best scorers stay more efficient than the rest: more points per shot in all 30 seasons.
  • A scoring title is volume, not always efficiency: 7 of 30 leaders shot below their league, while Stephen Curry in 2015-16 shot 12.8 points above it.
